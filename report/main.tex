\section{Integration Management}
Integration Management includes all of the processes required to unify, coordinate and manage all project elements to completion. This section describes how integration activities will be accomplished for the \textit{Business Sustainability Management Platform} delivered by Group 5 to Luckin Coffee.

\subsection{Integration Management Overview and Approach}
The primary objective of integration management for this project is to coordinate six work packages and unify ten functional modules to deliver a fully functional web-based ESG management platform for Luckin Coffee. The integration strategy employs a modular blueprint architecture implemented in Flask. This ensures each business module, such as carbon tracking and waste management, operates independently while sharing a unified data layer, authentication system, and frontend framework. All subsidiary plans are integrated into this comprehensive project management plan. The scope baseline encompasses the ten functional modules detailed in the System Documentation. The schedule baseline aligns with a 16-week timeline from March to June, with six milestone gates as defined in Section~4. The cost baseline covers internal labour, infrastructure provisioning, third-party service procurement, and ESG consulting fees, denominated in CNY and managed under the EVM framework defined in Section~5.

\subsection{Project Team Structure and Governance}
The project adopts a strong matrix organisational structure, granting the project manager moderate to high authority over resource allocation and task prioritisation. The internal delivery team consists of six members covering project management, architecture, backend, frontend, visualisation, and quality engineering, supplemented by an external ESG compliance consultant engaged for Weeks~2--4. The client side is represented by the Head of Luckin Coffee IT Department (project sponsor) and the Head of Luckin Coffee ESG Department (business owner). Cloud infrastructure is procured from a qualified Luckin Coffee supplier; the ESG reporting framework follows GRI Standards, UN SDG~12, and ISO~14064. The corporate governance structure, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:team_structure}, includes a Project Steering Committee chaired by the sponsor, which conducts biweekly reviews and approves baseline change requests escalated by the Change Control Board.

\subsection{Direct and Manage Project Execution}
Execution activities are driven by a structured workflow where all code and documentation are managed via GitLab. A main branch is maintained for stable releases alongside feature branches for specific work packages, with peer reviews required before merging. Weekly synchronisation meetings are held every Monday to review progress, identify blockers, and align upcoming tasks. Work performance data, including completed tasks, bug resolution rates, and test coverage, is collected weekly and integrated into status reports. Approved change requests are assigned to relevant work package leads, and their implementation is verified by the project manager before closure. The Project Management Information System (PMIS) comprises GitLab for version control and CI/CD, Jira for task and defect tracking, Confluence for collaborative documentation, Tencent Meeting for synchronous communication, Enterprise WeChat for day-to-day messaging, and Prometheus--Grafana for runtime monitoring of the deployed platform.

\subsection{Monitor and Control Project Work}
Project performance is continuously monitored against the 16-week schedule baseline. Key performance indicators include schedule variance, defect density, and cross-module integration resolution time. Earned Value Management (EVM) principles are applied to quantify progress, triggering corrective actions if the Schedule Performance Index (SPI) falls below 0.9 for two consecutive weeks. A three-tiered information flow ensures consistent reporting: raw work performance data is analyzed into performance information, which is then compiled into weekly status reports for the team and monthly updates for the sponsor. When deviations occur, the root cause is analyzed to develop corrective actions that minimize schedule disruption. Furthermore, technical risks and data anomalies are tracked during weekly reviews, utilizing the platform's built-in anomaly detection module to maintain data integrity.

\subsection{Change Management}
The formal change control process governs all modifications affecting the project's scope, schedule, or quality baselines. Any team member can identify a change, which must be documented via a GitLab Issue template. The relevant work package lead then conducts a comprehensive impact analysis. The Change Control Board (CCB), chaired by the project manager, reviews these requests during weekly Wednesday meetings. Minor changes, such as defect repairs, are approved by the project manager, while major scope additions require sponsor authorization. Emergency critical bug fixes may be implemented immediately but must undergo CCB review within three business days. All changes are tracked in a living change log, ensuring that only approved modifications are integrated into the main codebase.

\subsection{Project Close Out}
The project close-out phase ensures a systematic transition of the final deliverables to the sponsor. This process begins with scope verification to confirm all baseline requirements are met, followed by a final system quality acceptance test. Upon successful validation, the fully functional platform, comprehensive system documentation, user manuals, and presentation materials are formally transitioned to the sponsor, culminating in a signed acceptance certificate. Following the handover, team members are released from their primary responsibilities but remain available for a two-week support period. All project artifacts, including source code, design documents, and test results, are archived in the GitLab repository for future reference. Finally, a post-mortem meeting is conducted to document lessons learned, evaluating the effectiveness of the modular architecture and team structure to update organizational process assets.

\subsection{Scope Management Plan Overview}
This section establishes the framework for managing the scope of the Business Sustainability Management Platform, ensuring the project delivers all necessary features without unauthorized additions. The primary objectives are to clearly define product and project boundaries, standardize requirements collection, and prevent scope creep. The project adheres to standard scope management processes, encompassing scope planning, requirements collection, scope definition, Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) creation, scope validation, and scope control. The Project Manager oversees the entire process and chairs the Change Control Board, while specific team members, such as Gong Zhijian and Yao Tianren, lead frontend integration and requirements analysis, respectively. \subsection{Requirements Management}
Requirements are systematically categorized into business, stakeholder, solution, transition, project, and quality requirements. Business requirements focus on centralized ESG data management and compliance with GRI Standards. Stakeholder needs range from simple data entry for store staff to comprehensive analytics for headquarters managers. Solution requirements dictate the functional modules, such as carbon tracking and supplier assessment, alongside non-functional targets like supporting 1000 concurrent users and ensuring sub-two-second page load times. These requirements are elicited through interviews with restaurant managers, focus groups, prototyping, and document analysis. Prioritization follows the MoSCoW method, distinguishing essential features like carbon tracking from optional enhancements like OCR-assisted input. A Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM) is maintained to link each requirement to its source, WBS component, and test case, ensuring comprehensive coverage and facilitating impact analysis during change requests.

\subsection{Project Scope Statement}
The primary objective is to develop and deploy a web-based Business Sustainability Management Platform for multi-store restaurant chains by June 30, 2026. The product scope encompasses core modules for carbon tracking, waste management, and ESG governance, featuring role-based access control. The project scope includes all design, development, testing, deployment, and documentation activities. Out-of-scope items include native mobile application development, real-time IoT sensor integration, legacy data migration, and long-term post-delivery maintenance. Deliverables are accepted based on the successful implementation of all functional requirements, resolution of critical bugs, and successful deployment on the designated virtual machine. The project is constrained by a fixed June 2026 deadline, a five-member team, and the mandatory use of the Flask and MySQL technology stack, assuming continuous availability of team members and virtual machine infrastructure.

\subsection{Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)}
The Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) decomposes the project scope into manageable work packages, adhering to the 100\% rule to ensure all project work is captured. The decomposition is limited to seven hierarchical levels, with the lowest level representing work packages that require 8 to 80 hours of effort. A hierarchical encoding system is utilized, starting from the project level down to specific tasks. For instance, Work Package 1.4.1 involves developing the carbon emission tracking module, which includes implementing frontend pages, integrating backend APIs, and conducting module tests. The approved Project Scope Statement, WBS, and WBS Dictionary collectively form the scope baseline, which is strictly controlled against unauthorized changes.

\subsection{Validate Scope}
Scope validation formalizes the acceptance of completed project deliverables. Once the development team completes a deliverable and performs internal quality control, it is submitted to the client representative for review. The client evaluates the deliverable against predefined acceptance criteria. If accepted, a formal acceptance document is signed; if rejected, the team addresses the provided feedback and resubmits the deliverable. This process involves the Project Manager coordinating the review, the Development Team demonstrating the functionality, and the Client Representative making the acceptance decision. All acceptance documentation, including signed forms and test reports, is archived in the project repository.

\subsection{Control Scope}
Scope control involves continuously monitoring the project status to manage changes to the scope baseline. Progress is assessed weekly by comparing actual completed work against the WBS, with any variance exceeding 5\% triggering a formal review. All proposed scope modifications must follow a strict change control process: a written request is submitted, evaluated for impact by the Project Manager, and subsequently reviewed by the Change Control Board (CCB). To prevent scope creep, all client requests for new features require formal CCB approval. Similarly, gold plating is strictly prohibited; team members must not implement unauthorized enhancements without submitting them through the formal change request process.

\section{Schedule Management}

\subsection{Schedule Management Plan}

The Schedule Management Plan defines how the project schedule will be planned, developed, monitored, and controlled throughout the project lifecycle. It establishes the methods, tools, responsibilities, and procedures used to manage schedule activities and track project progress. The main purpose of this plan is to ensure that the project remains on schedule and that any delays or deviations can be identified and addressed in a timely manner.

\subsubsection{Milestone Progress Measurement}

Progress is formally assessed at each of the six milestones during the Friday team meeting. A milestone is recorded as complete only when all acceptance criteria for that phase---as specified in the WBS Dictionary---are fully satisfied. Between milestones, weekly task completion is compared against the Jira board to provide an ongoing progress signal.

\subsubsection{Methods and Tools for Schedule Performance Comparison}

The project uses three complementary methods to compare actual performance to planned performance:

\textbf{Gantt Chart.} The primary schedule tool is Jira (Timeline view), where a Gantt chart displays all activities on the vertical axis, dates on the horizontal axis, and activity durations as horizontal bars placed according to planned start and finish dates. Actual completion dates are logged weekly, making deviations immediately visible. The Gantt chart is updated every Friday before 17:00 and serves as the shared reference for all team members.

\textbf{Critical Path Method (CPM).} During schedule development, the Critical Path Method was applied to identify the longest path through the project network---the sequence of activities that determines the minimum possible project duration. Activities on the critical path carry zero total float, because any delay directly extends the project end date. Activities off the critical path carry total float and free float, providing scheduling flexibility. CPM guides prioritization: deviations on critical-path activities (e.g., backend API development, core module integration) trigger immediate corrective action, while deviations on non-critical activities are evaluated against their remaining float before action is taken.

\textbf{Schedule Variance Tracking.} Consistent with the EVM principles applied in Section 2.5, Schedule Variance (\(SV = EV - PV\)) and the Schedule Performance Index (\(SPI = EV / PV\)) are calculated weekly. An SPI below 0.9 for two consecutive weeks triggers a formal corrective action review.

\subsection{Sequence Activities}
% ============================================================

In this process, the relationships and dependencies between project activities are identified and arranged in a logical order. This helps the project team organise tasks more efficiently, coordinate parallel activities, and reduce unnecessary delays during the project lifecycle.

\subsubsection{Tools and Techniques for Sequence Activities}

\textbf{Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM).} This method is used to create a project schedule model by representing activities as nodes and linking them with logical dependencies to illustrate the order in which the activities should be carried out. Four logical relationships are used:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Finish-to-Start (FS)}: the successor activity cannot begin until the predecessor activity is complete.
    \item \textbf{Start-to-Start (SS)}: the successor activity cannot begin until the predecessor activity has started.
    \item \textbf{Finish-to-Finish (FF)}: the successor activity cannot finish until the predecessor activity has finished.
    \item \textbf{Start-to-Finish (SF)}: the successor activity cannot finish until the predecessor activity has started.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Dependency Attributes.} Activities are classified according to the following dependency types:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Mandatory Dependency}: inherent in the nature of the work and cannot be altered.
    \item \textbf{Discretionary Dependency}: established based on best practice or team preference.
    \item \textbf{External Dependency}: involves a relationship between project activities and non-project activities.
    \item \textbf{Internal Dependency}: involves a precedence relationship between project activities.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Leads and Lags.}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Leads}: allow a successor activity to begin before its predecessor activity is fully completed, helping reduce the overall project duration through overlapping work. Leads are represented as negative values.
    \item \textbf{Lags}: introduce a delay between two related activities, requiring a certain amount of time to pass before the successor activity can begin. Lags are represented as positive values.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Control Schedule}
% ============================================================

Schedule control is the process of monitoring the status of project activities to update project progress and manage changes to the schedule baseline to achieve the plan. The key benefit of this process is that it provides the means to recognize deviation from the plan and take corrective and preventive actions, thus minimizing risk.

\subsubsection{Corrective Action When Performance Deviates}

When actual progress deviates from the planned schedule, corrective and preventive actions are applied according to the severity of the deviation. The primary recovery tools are two schedule compression techniques---crashing and fast tracking.

\textbf{Minor deviation} (individual tasks delayed by 1--3 days, with no impact on the critical path): The affected work package lead is required to promptly report the delay. Upon receiving the notification, the project manager initiates an ad hoc coordination meeting to review the cause and reallocate tasks within the team if necessary. No formal change request is required.

\textbf{Moderate deviation} (a critical-path task at risk, or SPI $<$ 0.9 for two consecutive weeks): A corrective action review is convened within 48 hours. The project manager and relevant work package leads conduct a root cause analysis. Two schedule compression techniques are applied as appropriate:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Fast tracking}: Activities originally planned sequentially are performed in parallel for part of their duration---for example, conducting integration and unit testing while development is still in progress. Fast tracking may introduce rework risk and is applied only when task overlap is technically feasible.
    \item \textbf{Crashing}: Additional resources are assigned to activities to shorten their duration---for example, reallocating a team member from a lower-priority work package or temporarily engaging outsourced developers. Crashing may increase project costs and coordination overhead, so it is applied selectively with careful cost control.
\end{itemize}

Contingency reserves built into the schedule baseline may be drawn upon to absorb moderate delays without triggering a formal baseline revision.

\textbf{Severe deviation} (a milestone missed, or SPI $<$ 0.9 for four or more consecutive weeks): Failure to achieve any milestone is considered a severe deviation and requires a formal schedule revision to be initiated.

\subsubsection{Schedule Modification and Commitment}

The schedule baseline may be formally revised when a milestone is missed, when an approved scope change materially affects the critical path, or when a verified technical blocker cannot be absorbed by available float or contingency reserve. The project manager prepares the revised schedule in Jira, presents it at the Wednesday CCB meeting, and obtains approval per the authority levels in Section 2.6. Each work package lead acknowledges revised task dates within 24 hours via Jira comment. The previous baseline is archived in GitLab as a tagged release. Formal revisions are limited to three over the project lifecycle.

\subsection{Schedule Responsibility and Accountability}
% ============================================================

All team members are responsible for completing assigned activities according to the approved schedule baseline and for updating task progress in Jira on time. Each work package lead is responsible for monitoring the progress of activities within their area and reporting any potential delays during the weekly project meeting.

If an activity is expected to miss its planned finish date, the responsible team member must inform the project manager as early as possible and provide the reason for the delay together with a proposed recovery plan. Failure to communicate schedule risks in a timely manner will be recorded during project reviews.

When schedule deviations affect milestone dates or critical-path activities, the project manager will review the cause of the delay with the relevant work package lead. Corrective actions---such as task reassignment, fast tracking, or schedule compression---may then be applied to recover the schedule.

\section{Cost Management}
% ============================================================

Project Cost Management for the Luckin Coffee Sustainability Platform covers four processes: Plan Cost Management, Estimate Costs, Determine Budget, and Control Costs. The goal is to ensure the project is completed within the approved budget while maintaining full transparency on cost performance at every milestone gate.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Plan Cost Management}
% ============================================================

The Cost Management Plan defines the following parameters that govern how all cost-related activities are conducted:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Units of measure}: Chinese Yuan (CNY) is used for all monetary costs; person-days is used for labour effort to eliminate the effect of currency fluctuations when comparing workload across phases.
    \item \textbf{Level of precision and accuracy}: all monetary cost figures are rounded to the nearest hundred yuan (e.g., ¥10,049 is recorded as ¥10,000); estimates carry an acceptance range of $\pm$10\%.
    \item \textbf{Organisational procedures link}: costs are tracked at the WBS work package level. Each of the 32 activities is mapped to a control account maintained in the project cost tracking spreadsheet in GitLab.
    \item \textbf{Control threshold}: a Cost Variance (CV) or Schedule Variance (SV) exceeding $\pm$10\% of the planned baseline triggers a formal corrective action review, consistent with the EVM thresholds applied in Section 5.4.
    \item \textbf{Rules of performance measurement}: Earned Value Management (EVM) is applied weekly to assess cost efficiency. The CPI and SPI are the primary performance indicators reported at each Friday team meeting and at every milestone gate.
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================
\subsection{Estimate Costs}
% ============================================================

Cost estimation for this project was conducted during the planning phase by the project manager in collaboration with all work package leads. The team applied the practical software cost estimation process structured in five steps:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Task decomposition}: the 32 activities in the WBS are used as the unit of cost estimation. Each activity is assigned to the responsible work package lead, who provides the cost estimate for their scope.
    \item \textbf{Estimate the cost of each task}: each work package lead estimates the workload ($E_i$, in person-days) using the Expert Estimation (Delphi) method. Each estimator provides three values---optimistic (O), most likely (M), and pessimistic (P)---and the expected workload is derived using the Beta Distribution formula: $E = (O + 4M + P) / 6$. Task cost ($C_i$) is then calculated as $C_i = E_i \times$ human cost parameter (¥/person-day).
    \item \textbf{Direct cost estimation}: development cost is aggregated across all 32 activities using the bottom-up estimating approach. Management and quality overhead is added at 20--25\% of development workload, yielding the total direct cost.
    \item \textbf{Indirect cost estimation}: indirect costs (infrastructure, tools, communications, and administrative support) are calculated as 30\% of total direct cost (indirect cost coefficient = 0.3).
    \item \textbf{Total project cost estimate}: Total estimated cost = workload $\times$ cost coefficient, where cost coefficient = human cost parameter $\times$ (1 + indirect cost coefficient).
\end{itemize}

All activity cost estimates are documented in the project cost tracking spreadsheet stored in GitLab, including the estimation method used, the three-point values, and the derived expected cost. Cost estimates were reviewed and approved at the M1 milestone gate (March 28, 2026) and will be re-evaluated at each subsequent milestone if material scope changes occur.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Determine Budget}
% ============================================================

The project budget is determined by aggregating all activity cost estimates and adding reserve provisions. The budget is structured in the following layers:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Activity Cost Estimates}: the rolled-up direct and indirect costs from Section 5.2, summed across all 32 WBS activities.
    \item \textbf{Contingency Reserve}: an amount corresponding to identified ``known-unknown'' risks (consistent with the 15-day schedule contingency reserve defined in Section~\ref{subsec:estimate_duration}), held within the Cost Baseline and managed by the project manager. This reserve may be drawn upon without a formal change request when cost overruns fall within this buffer.
    \item \textbf{Management Reserve}: a budget held by the project sponsor for unforeseen ``unknown-unknown'' risks that cannot be identified at planning time. The management reserve is excluded from the Cost Baseline and requires sponsor approval before use.
\end{itemize}

The sum of all activity cost estimates and the contingency reserve constitutes the \textbf{Cost Baseline (BAC)}. The Cost Baseline is displayed as an S-curve that plots cumulative planned expenditure against the project timeline, tied to the six milestone dates listed in Table~\ref{tab:milestone_schedule}. The S-curve is maintained in the project cost tracking spreadsheet in GitLab and serves as the reference against which actual expenditure is compared each week.

Project funding requirements are released by milestone gate: the sponsor confirms the release of funds for the next phase at each milestone review. The Cost Baseline may only be revised through formal change control procedures, consistent with the authority levels defined in Section 2.6. Formal revisions are limited to three over the project lifecycle.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Control Costs}
% ============================================================

Cost control is carried out throughout the project lifecycle using Earned Value Management (EVM). The project manager calculates the following EVM indicators weekly and reports them at the Friday team meeting and at each milestone gate:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{PV (Planned Value)} = BAC $\times$ planned \% complete, derived from the Cost Baseline S-curve at the current data date.
    \item \textbf{EV (Earned Value)} = BAC $\times$ actual \% complete, where actual \% complete is assessed based on milestone acceptance criteria and Jira task completion status.
    \item \textbf{AC (Actual Cost)}: the total expenditure incurred to date, recorded weekly in the cost tracking spreadsheet.
\end{itemize}

From these three values, the following performance metrics and variances are derived each week:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{CV (Cost Variance)} = EV $-$ AC. A negative CV indicates the project is over budget for work completed.
    \item \textbf{SV (Schedule Variance)} = EV $-$ PV. A negative SV indicates the project is behind schedule.
    \item \textbf{CPI (Cost Performance Index)} = EV / AC. A CPI below 1.0 indicates cost overrun; above 1.0 indicates cost underrun.
    \item \textbf{SPI (Schedule Performance Index)} = EV / PV. An SPI below 1.0 indicates less work was completed than planned.
\end{itemize}

The following thresholds govern when corrective action is required:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{CPI $\geq$ 0.9}: no corrective action required; performance is monitored weekly.
    \item \textbf{CPI $<$ 0.9 for two consecutive weeks}: a corrective action review is convened within 48 hours. The project manager and relevant work package leads conduct a root cause analysis and adjust resource allocation, reduce scope within approved boundaries, or apply schedule compression techniques to recover cost and schedule performance. Contingency reserve may be drawn upon if the overrun falls within the reserved amount.
    \item \textbf{CPI $<$ 0.9 for four or more consecutive weeks, or a milestone missed due to cost issues}: a formal Cost Baseline revision is prepared by the project manager, presented at the Wednesday CCB meeting, and submitted to the sponsor for approval per the authority levels in Section 2.6.
\end{itemize}

Forecasting is applied each week to project the final cost at completion. When a persistent CPI trend is observed, the following EVM forecasting metrics are calculated:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{EAC (Estimate at Completion)} = BAC / CPI
    \item \textbf{ETC (Estimate to Complete)} = EAC $-$ AC
    \item \textbf{VAC (Variance at Completion)} = BAC $-$ EAC
    \item \textbf{TCPI (To-Complete Performance Index)} = (BAC $-$ EV) / (BAC $-$ AC)
\end{itemize}

The TCPI is monitored alongside the CPI to understand the cost efficiency level that must be maintained on all remaining work to complete within the approved budget. If the TCPI significantly exceeds the current CPI, the project manager escalates to the sponsor for a budget revision review.

All EVM data (PV, EV, AC, CV, SV, CPI, SPI, EAC, ETC, VAC, TCPI) is recorded weekly in the project cost tracking spreadsheet in GitLab. The S-curve is updated every Friday before 17:00. A full cost performance review is conducted at each of the six milestone gates, with findings documented in the milestone review report submitted to the sponsor.

\subsection{Plan Risk Management}

The Risk Management Plan defines how risk activities will be conducted on this project. It is structured around the seven planning elements taught in the course: methodology, roles and responsibilities, budgeting, timing, risk categories, definitions of probability and impact, and the probability and impact matrix.

\subsubsection{Methodology, Roles, and Timing}

\textbf{Methodology.} Risk management on this project follows the PMBOK process model presented in the course. The team applies the following tools and techniques across the six processes: brainstorming, SWOT analysis, structured interviews, and root cause analysis for identification; the Probability and Impact (P--I) Matrix for qualitative analysis; Expected Monetary Value (EMV) analysis with decision tree and Sensitivity Analysis (Tornado Diagram) for quantitative analysis; the eight PMBOK response strategies for response planning; and risk reassessment, risk audits, variance and trend analysis, technical performance measurement, reserve analysis, and meetings for control. All risk records are maintained in a Risk Register stored in Confluence and version-controlled in GitLab.

\textbf{Roles and Responsibilities.} The Project Manager is the overall risk owner, accountable for risk planning, escalation, and reserve approval below the management threshold. Each work package lead is the primary risk owner for risks within their scope and is responsible for monitoring triggers and executing approved response actions. Every team member is required to identify and report new risks during the daily stand-up. The ESG Compliance Consultant supports identification and analysis of compliance-related risks during Weeks~2--4. The Project Sponsor (Head of Luckin Coffee IT Department) approves use of the management reserve and authorises responses to severe risks beyond the project manager's authority.

\textbf{Timing.} Risk management activities are integrated into the project rhythm: a five-minute risk check is included in every daily stand-up; the Risk Register is reviewed and updated during every Friday team meeting (15 minutes); a full risk reassessment, including P--I matrix re-scoring, is conducted at every milestone gate (M1--M6); a formal risk audit is performed at the M3 and M5 gates by an independent reviewer.

\textbf{Effort Allocation.} Approximately 5\% of total project effort is budgeted for risk management activities, distributed across identification, analysis, response planning, and control.

\subsubsection{Risk Budgeting}

Risk budgeting is integrated with the cost and schedule baselines defined in Sections~4 and~5. Two reserve layers are maintained:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Contingency Reserve} (within the cost and schedule baselines, managed by the Project Manager): covers identified ``known-unknown'' risks listed in the Risk Register. Schedule contingency is 15~working days, as defined in Section~\ref{subsec:estimate_duration}; cost contingency is sized to cover the aggregate Expected Monetary Value of negative risks plus a 20\% safety margin (see Section~11.4). The Project Manager may draw on this reserve without sponsor approval, provided the use is documented in the Risk Register and reported at the next milestone gate.
    \item \textbf{Management Reserve} (outside the baselines, controlled by the Project Sponsor): covers ``unknown-unknown'' risks that cannot be identified at planning time. Schedule management reserve is 8~working days. Use of the management reserve requires a formal change request approved by the sponsor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Identify Risks}

Risk identification is conducted iteratively throughout the project lifecycle. The team applies the following four information-gathering and analysis techniques taught in the course:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Brainstorming} (the first technique recommended in the course): the entire team holds a structured brainstorming session at project kickoff and at the start of each major phase. Risks are captured without judgement, then grouped against the RBS categories.
    \item \textbf{SWOT Analysis}: applied during the planning phase to systematically examine project Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. Threats and Weaknesses translate into negative risks; Opportunities translate into positive risks (handled with exploit/enhance/share strategies, see Section~11.5).
    \item \textbf{Interviews}: structured interviews are conducted with the Head of Luckin Coffee IT Department, the Head of Luckin Coffee ESG Department, and the external ESG Compliance Consultant during Week~1, focusing on customer expectations, regulatory updates, and historical project pain points.
    \item \textbf{Root Cause Analysis}: applied to recurring defects and identified risks during the execution phase, using the 5-Why method and the fishbone diagram introduced in Section~7. The output feeds back into the Risk Register as updated triggers and improved response plans.
\end{itemize}

The output of this process is the \textbf{Risk Register} (Table~\ref{tab:risk_register}), which records 15~identified risks distributed across the three RBS categories (5 Technical, 5 External, 5 Organizational), including 12 negative risks (threats) and 3 positive risks (opportunities).

\subsection{Perform Quantitative Risk Analysis}

Quantitative risk analysis is performed on the high-priority risks identified in Section~11.3 to produce numerical inputs for reserve sizing and decision making. Two of the three quantitative techniques taught in the course are applied: \textbf{Expected Monetary Value (EMV) analysis} and \textbf{Sensitivity Analysis (Tornado Diagram)}. Data is gathered through structured interviews with the Project Manager and work package leads, supplemented by expert judgement from the ESG Consultant.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity Analysis (Tornado Diagram)}

A Sensitivity Analysis is conducted to determine which uncertainties have the greatest potential impact on the project's overall objectives. The team estimates the project's total cost variance under best-case and worst-case scenarios for each high-priority risk, holding all other variables constant. The results, in descending order of sensitivity, are summarised below (a Tornado Diagram visualisation will be added before the M2 milestone gate):

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{E1 --- Luckin Coffee scope expansion}: cost swing range \textpm¥45{,}000. The largest single source of variance.
    \item \textbf{T3 --- Cross-module integration defects}: cost swing range \textpm¥28{,}000.
    \item \textbf{T1 --- Architecture instability under high load}: cost swing range \textpm¥22{,}000.
    \item \textbf{E3 --- ESG regulatory framework update}: cost swing range \textpm¥18{,}000.
    \item \textbf{O2 --- Team-member partial reallocation}: cost swing range \textpm¥14{,}000.
\end{enumerate}

The sensitivity analysis confirms that customer-driven scope expansion (E1) and integration defects (T3) are the dominant drivers of cost uncertainty, justifying the prioritised response strategies in Section~11.5.

\subsection{Control Risks}

Risk control is performed throughout the project lifecycle using the six tools and techniques taught in the course:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Risk Reassessment}: the Risk Register is reassessed at every Friday team meeting (15~minutes) and at every milestone gate. New risks are added; closed risks are archived; P--I scores of remaining risks are updated based on latest information.
    \item \textbf{Risk Audits}: an independent reviewer (the Quality Manager from Luckin Coffee's IT Department) conducts a formal risk audit at the M3 and M5 gates, examining the effectiveness of risk responses and the discipline of risk identification. Audit findings are documented in the Risk Register.
    \item \textbf{Variance and Trend Analysis}: weekly Cost Performance Index (CPI) and Schedule Performance Index (SPI), defined in Sections~5.4 and~4.1, are tracked as risk indicators. A two-week trend with CPI or SPI $<$0.9 triggers a focused risk reassessment, since negative cost or schedule trends often signal an emerging risk.
    \item \textbf{Technical Performance Measurement}: technical metrics including API response time, page load time, integration test pass rate, and defect density (defined in Section~6.1) are measured at each milestone gate. Underperformance against targets is escalated as a potential risk trigger.
    \item \textbf{Reserve Analysis}: the remaining contingency reserve (in CNY and working days) is reviewed weekly. If reserve consumption exceeds the linear schedule (e.g., 40\% of reserve consumed by M2 when only 25\% of work is complete), the Project Manager initiates a formal corrective review with the sponsor.
    \item \textbf{Status Meetings}: a five-minute risk discussion is included in every daily stand-up; risk status is a standing agenda item at every Friday team meeting and every milestone review with Luckin Coffee.
\end{enumerate}

The Risk Register is the single source of truth for all risk control activities, maintained in Confluence with version control via GitLab. Every approved response action, every triggered contingent plan, and every reserve drawdown is recorded against its risk ID for end-of-project lessons-learned analysis.
